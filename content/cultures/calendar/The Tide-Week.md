The tide on Areshnaat repeats itself every seven days, exactly, and has always
done so. The tide-week is the oldest unit of reckoning on the water, and on
most coasts it is older than any calendar laid over it.

## Why seven

[[The Four Moons]] are not free to wander. Red, Green and Blue are locked to
one another — Red circling four times for Green's two and Blue's one — and a
locked system keeps time. Counted against the turning of the world rather than
against the stars, in seven days:

| Moon | works the water |
|---|---|
| Red | three times |
| Green | five times |
| Blue | six times |

Three, five and six: whole numbers, every time. On the eighth day the three
stand as they stood on the first, and the week begins again with nothing left
over. White is too far out and too slow to disturb this; what White does is
swell the whole week and then slacken it, on its own month.

## How it stacks

- **7 days** — one tide-week. The tide repeats.
- **4 tide-weeks** — one month, which is also White full to full: 28 days.
- **13 months** — 364 days, and fifty-two tide-weeks.
- **One day over.** The year is 365 and a quarter; the months account for 364.
  That day belongs to no month and no week.

## What it is worth

The water here is fast and it is heavy — four moons pulling at once — but it
is not unreadable. That combination is the whole of a pilot's trade, and it is
why tide-knowledge on Areshnaat is not seamanship but property.

A passage that is safe on the third day of the week will drown a ship on the
fifth, and will be safe again seven days later. Anyone who has computed the
tables can sail it. Anyone who has not must wait, or guess, and the guess is
usually fatal. So the tables are kept, copied under seal, and not sold.

The goblin **Companhia** is built on exactly this. Its fleets are ordinary; its
almanacs are not. Charters, port fees, interest and wages are all reckoned by
the tide-week rather than the month, because the week is what the sea actually
obeys — and the house that issues the week issues the terms.

## The day that is owed to nothing

The leftover day sits outside every month and every week. Contracts reckoned in
tide-weeks do not run on it, debts reckoned in months do not accrue, and a
charter that ends on the last day of the thirteenth month has not yet begun the
next. What is done with that day — kept as a holiday, used as a loophole, or
quietly owned — is not settled.
